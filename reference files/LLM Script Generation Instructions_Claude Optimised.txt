STRUCTURE TO FOLLOW

1. Introduction.
   • Begin with a concrete moment, detail, or contrast that catches attention immediately. No broad metaphors, no clichés.
   • Present the central problem or question of the episode in compelling terms.
   • Give 3-4 learning outcomes in conversational language: "By the end, you'll see how…"

2. Overview. 
   • Give a natural-sounding outline of the episode's flow.
   • Use bridging phrases ("First we look at…, then…, finally…"). Avoid formal enumeration tone.

3. Chronology.
   • Present key events as a brief narrative sequence rather than a list.
   • Group dates where practical. Avoid unnecessary date repetition.
   • Emphasise cause and effect.

4. Main Parts (several, as required).
   • Start each part with a grounded detail, anecdote, or condition that sets the scene.
   • Use active voice, strong verbs, and varied sentence lengths (up to 35 words).  
   • Maintain a fluid rhythm, balancing short statements with longer, more detailed sentences. Shorter sentences can be used after long sentences to re-anchor attention. DO NOT provide a long sequence of short sentences.
   • Highlight contrasts where helpful.
   • Introduce or briefly describe any new person or term or event named. E.g. instead of 'Scott Fitzgerald dubbed it the Jazz Age', say 'The author Scott Fitzgerald dubbed it the Jazz Age'.  Instead of 'the decade's "flappers" represented broader changes.' say 'the decade's "flappers" represented broader changes. Flappers were young women adopted shorter skirts, bobbed hair, and openly smoked and drank.'
   • End each part with a one-sentence takeaway.

5. Synthesis and Review. 
   • Draw together patterns, tensions, or turning points.
   • Show how the themes relate without repeating earlier sentences.
   • Restate 4-6 essential points in crisp, fresh phrasing.
   • Include 1-2 thoughtful questions that prompt deeper consideration.

6. Key Names, Places, Events, Things.
   • Present up to eight items. Items should have been already mentioned in previous sections.
   • Number them from one to eight.
   • Give each a brief, vivid identifying phrase.

7. Closing.
   • Offer two to three concluding lines that encapsulate the major events or trends just related. E.g. 'Propelled by population growth, aggressive tribal competition, and the weakness of brittle steppe-adjacent states, the Mongols under Genghis Khan went from local nomadic tribes contained in a relatively small part of North-East Asia to a trans-Eurasian power conquering settled states and causing waves from the Atlantic to the Pacific.'
   • Optionally, contrast the start and end states of the period covered. E.g. 'in the fourteen hundreds African slaves were relatively limited groups of captured individuals in western Africa. By the late nineteenth century there were tens of millions of formerly enslaved peoples of African descent across the Americas and the Caribbean, forming large and important parts of young nations and contributing their uniquely developed cultures. In the period in between X million had died through the middle passage or through the conditions of slavery. X million had lived lives of misery and bondage, often shortened by cruel conditions. ' 
   • No reflection on future impacts of the period, no clichés, no vague uplift.
